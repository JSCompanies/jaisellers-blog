History often gives us a photograph of a moment. It does not always show us the thousands of hours that made the moment possible.

Medgar Evers understood the work beneath the headlines. As a civil rights organizer in Mississippi, he helped confront discrimination through organizing, investigation, voter-registration efforts, and persistent public pressure. He worked in a place where the risks were not abstract.

He was murdered in 1963. Yet to remember him only through the violence that ended his life would be to overlook the purpose that filled it.

I am moved by the kind of courage that gets up the next morning and does the work again.

It is not always theatrical. Sometimes it looks like returning a phone call. Documenting a wrong. Helping a family understand a process. Keeping a record so that a person cannot simply be erased. Showing up when the attention has moved elsewhere.

That is not small work. In many cases, it is the structure that allows larger change to happen.

When I think about the mission behind Justice Systems Initiative, I think about those invisible hours. A family may never know who clarified a rule, requested a record, found a resource, or made a process more humane. The impact can be life-changing even when nobody remembers the name.

I do not compare my own efforts to Evers's sacrifice. I take from his life a more modest and more demanding lesson: that the worth of public service cannot be measured by applause.

We need people who speak from the podium. We also need people who build the steps, open the doors, check the facts, and remain after the crowd goes home.

Medgar Evers deserves to be remembered as more than a name between better-known chapters of civil rights history. He reminds us that justice is often won through the accumulation of ordinary acts performed under extraordinary pressure.

**The work that matters most is not always the work that history remembers first.**
