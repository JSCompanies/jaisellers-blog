---
title: The Scarlet Letter We Still Sew
date: 2026-07-09
summary: We tell people coming home from prison to change their lives, then build a system that makes change impossible.
tag: Reentry
---

I sat across from a man last month who had done everything right. Eighteen months clean inside. GED finished. A trade certification he earned in a program most guys skip. He walked out with a folder of paperwork and a bus ticket, and by week two he had been turned down for eleven apartments and nine jobs — not because he lied about his record, but because he told the truth about it.

That's the part nobody wants to say out loud: we didn't build a system to help people come home. We built one to make sure they never fully do.

We hand a man a release date and call it freedom. Then we hand him a background check that follows him into every leasing office and every HR department for the rest of his life. We tell him to "do better" while zoning laws keep him out of whole neighborhoods, while landlords treat a felony record like a permanent character reference, while employers file his application in the trash before they read the second page. We ask him to build a stable life on a foundation we've made illegal for him to stand on.

> That's not a justice system. That's a scarlet letter with better branding.

**Scripture doesn't leave room for this.** The cities of refuge in Numbers 35 weren't theoretical — they were physical infrastructure, built and maintained, specifically so a person could return to a life that wasn't defined by their worst moment forever. God commanded shelter. We countered with credit checks.

This is the theology behind The Bridges Initiative. Transitional housing isn't charity — it's restitution for a system we all fund and rarely question. A roof and a job aren't a reward for good behavior; they're the baseline requirement for a human being to exist. When we withhold both and then act surprised by recidivism, we're not looking at a failure of the person. We're looking at a failure we designed.

Here's the policy reality underneath the theology: as many as 60 to 75 percent of formerly incarcerated people are still jobless a year after release, and housing instability is one of the strongest predictors of a return to custody. We know what works. Ban-the-box hiring. Fair-chance housing policy. Transitional housing with actual case management, not just a bed and a curfew. None of it is a mystery. It's a funding and will problem, not a knowledge problem.

So the question isn't whether we know how to fix this. It's whether we're willing to be inconvenienced by mercy.

The Bridges Initiative exists because someone has to be the exception to a system built on scarcity of second chances. If you're a landlord, an employer, a church, or a funder reading this — that someone can be you too.
