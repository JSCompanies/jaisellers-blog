There is a version of Jesus that asks very little of us. He is gentle, agreeable, and framed safely above the furniture. He comforts without confronting. He blesses whatever we have already decided to do.

I have loved that image at times. I understand why we reach for it. Life can be cruel, and the promise of being held by grace is no small thing.

But I keep returning to a more demanding question: **What if the Jesus who comforts us is also the Jesus who refuses to let us become comfortable with someone else's suffering?**

In the Gospels, he repeatedly moves toward people who have been pushed to the edges: people carrying illness, poverty, suspicion, shame, and reputations that polite society found inconvenient. Again and again, he treats the person in front of him as more important than the boundary that supposedly keeps them apart.

That is not a sentimental kind of love. It has consequences. It can interrupt a meal, challenge a rule, unsettle a crowd, or ask the powerful to give something up.

For me, faith cannot stop at being personally reassured that God loves me. It has to become a way of seeing other people. If my religion leaves me more comfortable with exclusion, contempt, or indifference, then I need to examine what I am worshiping.

I do not pretend that living this out is easy. I know the desire to be understood, the instinct to protect myself, and the exhaustion that can turn compassion into a slogan. But perhaps that is why Jesus still matters: not because he lets me feel finished, but because he calls me back to the work.

This first portrait in *Mosaic of Humanity* is not an attempt to settle what Jesus looked like. It is an invitation to look again at what his life might require.

**Love that never costs us anything may be kindness. Radical love asks whether we are willing to change the conditions that make kindness necessary.**
