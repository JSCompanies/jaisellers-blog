We know how to celebrate a dream. We are less practiced at paying its bill.

Martin Luther King Jr. has become one of the most familiar figures in American public memory. His image appears in classrooms, on posters, and at commemorations. His words are repeated by people who disagree profoundly about what justice requires.

But admiration is not the same as agreement. And remembrance is not the same as change.

King's public work was not simply a request that we be nicer to one another. It challenged laws, institutions, economic arrangements, and habits of power. He spoke of nonviolence, but nonviolence did not mean passivity. It required disciplined confrontation with injustice and a willingness to accept real personal risk.

I think about that whenever our conversations about equality become so polished that nobody has to change anything afterward.

It is easy to support fairness as an idea. It is harder to ask who has access to housing, counsel, education, work, healthcare, and a meaningful chance to begin again. It is harder still to examine the systems that decide who receives those things and who is asked to wait.

For me, that is where a dream becomes a responsibility.

In my own work, I am drawn to the space between honoring someone's story and building a system that can actually serve them. I do not want justice to exist only in a stirring speech or on a beautiful graphic. I want it to survive contact with budgets, policies, offices, and ordinary Tuesday mornings.

King's legacy also challenges our temptation to turn complicated people into flawless symbols. We do not need perfect heroes to learn from history. We need enough honesty to see both their courage and the unfinished work they left to us.

The question is not whether I find the dream beautiful. I do.

**The question is what I am willing to build so someone else can live inside it.**
