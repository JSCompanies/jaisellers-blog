A friend of mine got asked to step down from serving on a ministry team the same month she started grief counseling. Nobody framed it as punishment. It was "just not the right season." She hasn't been back to a church since. That's not a story about her losing her faith. That's a story about a system that couldn't tell the difference between someone who was struggling and someone who was disqualified.

That's the pattern this series is naming. Here are twenty places I see it show up.

**1. We weaponize truth instead of healing with it.**

Jesus rarely used truth as a hammer. He used it as a scalpel. Many churches have become very good at identifying sin and very poor at restoring sinners. We have confused conviction with condemnation.

Picture it: a Bible turned into a sword. A hospital with "Closed" hanging on the door. Bandages wrapped around a cross instead of nails.

**2. We celebrate forgiveness until someone we dislike needs it.**

Grace is beautiful when it costs us nothing. The real test comes when the person asking for mercy is LGBTQ+, formerly incarcerated, addicted, divorced, politically different, homeless, undocumented, or mentally ill. Mercy becomes selective.

Is it grace if everyone receiving it already looks like us?

**3. We worship certainty instead of humility.**

The modern church often speaks with absolute confidence on subjects Scripture itself treats with mystery. Jesus frequently answered questions with another question. We answer every question with a sermon.

**4. We have become collectors of enemies.**

Sometimes it seems Christians know more about who they're against than who they're for. Jesus spent far more time introducing people to the Kingdom than attacking Rome.

**5. We protect reputations more than people.**

Perhaps one of the greatest failures. Church scandals often reveal the same order of priorities: protect the pastor, protect the church, protect the brand. The victim becomes the problem. Mercy disappears.

**6. We preach transformation but fear transparency.**

People feel pressure to testify about victory. Few feel safe admitting they're still struggling. Church becomes a museum of polished saints instead of a hospital for wounded people.

**7. We have mistaken holiness for isolation.**

Jesus was accused of eating with sinners. Today some churches would question why He was there. Holiness wasn't avoiding broken people. It was bringing God into broken places.

**8. We confuse political power with spiritual authority.**

Whenever Christianity depends more on elections than on the Gospel, something has shifted. The early church had almost no political influence. Yet it changed the world.

**9. We preach generosity while hoarding comfort.**

Many churches raise millions for buildings while neighborhoods around them remain hungry. The question isn't whether churches should own property. It's whether our budgets reflect our theology.

**10. We are better at gatekeeping than welcoming.**

The disciples repeatedly asked, "Who belongs?" Jesus repeatedly answered, "Come."

**11. We quote Paul more than we imitate Christ.**

Paul matters. But sometimes Christians become experts in Paul's prohibitions while overlooking Jesus's posture. Jesus touched lepers. He defended adulterers from execution. He welcomed children, forgave enemies, restored failures.

**12. We confuse accountability with punishment.**

Mercy doesn't erase consequences. But neither does accountability require humiliation. The church often excels at public shame instead of private restoration.

**13. We mistake agreement for unity.**

Unity isn't everyone thinking alike. It's loving one another despite disagreement. The early church argued constantly. Yet they broke bread together.

**14. We have forgotten how to lament.**

Church services often leave little room for grief. Everything becomes victory, breakthrough, blessing. But Scripture contains entire books of lament. Mercy sometimes sounds like sitting in silence beside someone who's suffering.

**15. We love testimonies with happy endings.**

We celebrate "I was addicted," "I was healed," "I was restored." We don't know what to do with "I'm still depressed," "I'm still grieving," "I still have questions." Grace should have room for unfinished stories.

**16. We expect perfection from people while depending on perfection from Christ.**

This may be the greatest contradiction. We preach "no one is righteous," then expect church members to appear righteous all the time.

**17. We have made belonging the reward instead of the starting place.**

Jesus usually let people belong before they believed everything. Many churches reverse the order: believe, behave, then belong. Jesus often just said, "Come."

**18. Mercy has become a theology instead of a practice.**

We love discussing grace. We struggle extending it. Grace isn't measured by how loudly it's preached. It's measured by who feels safe walking through the doors.

**19. We fear questions more than hypocrisy.**

Churches often welcome certainty. Curiosity makes people nervous. Yet Thomas questioned. Jacob wrestled. David doubted. Jeremiah complained. Even Jesus cried out, "My God, why have you forsaken me?" Questions aren't the enemy of faith. Pretending not to have them might be.

**20. The church often asks people to carry burdens Christ already carried.**

People leave church believing they must earn acceptance, forgiveness, belonging, God's love. The Gospel says those were gifts before they became achievements.

---

None of this is written from the outside looking in. I've done more than one of these things myself. That's the point of naming them plainly instead of vaguely — you can't repent of something you won't name. Part three of this series is coming.
